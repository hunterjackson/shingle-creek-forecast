How the forecast works

At low and moderate flows the creek's level is set by Lake Tohopekaliga a few miles downstream, so the forecast works out two things: how much water the creek will carry, and where the lake will sit. Flow starts from a rainfall–runoff model of the 180-square-mile basin, driven by gridded rainfall, radar for the most recent day or two, and NOAA's GEFS rain forecast for the week ahead.

Machine-learning models then combine that with the morning gauge reading, the upstream gauge near Kissimmee, the levels of Lake Toho and East Lake Toho against their regulation schedule, and releases at structures S-59 and S-61. One model predicts the level directly; the other predicts flow and lake level and converts them to a creek level. The forecast is the average of the two.

The shaded likely range is set so the real level falls inside it about 80% of the time, judged against past forecasts that used the rain forecasts actually issued. Quiet days get a narrow range. From day 2 on, when rain or a big change is forecast, a separate model estimates how wide the range should be, so it widens most when a forecast is likely to miss, as during floods. For days 1 to 3 the range also widens whenever at least 5 mm of rain is forecast for the basin, because the timing and amount of rain is the biggest source of short-range misses. On 2024–2026, days with that much rain forecast landed inside the range 82% of the time on day 1 (61% before this change) and 80% on day 3 (73% before).

Lake Kissimmee, Lake Okeechobee, groundwater wells, evaporation forecasts, the National Water Model and the individual GEFS ensemble members were tested as extra inputs and left out because they didn't improve accuracy. Other model types (CatBoost, smaller-tree and linear-leaf LightGBM, an alternative rainfall–runoff model) were also tried; none beat the current pair.

This is an experimental, independent forecast, not an official one. For flood warnings, follow the National Weather Service in Melbourne and Osceola County Emergency Management.

Data sources

Everything is public and free. Each source is fetched on every run.

  • USGS 02264495 — Shingle Creek at Campbell, the gauge being forecast: level and flow, daily history and 15-minute readings.
  • USGS 02263800 — Shingle Creek at Airport near Kissimmee, the upstream gauge (flow).
  • SFWMD DBHYDRO — Lake Tohopekaliga and East Lake Toho levels, and releases at structures S-59 and S-61.
  • gridMET — gridded daily rainfall and evaporation demand since 1979, used for training and recent rain.
  • Iowa Environmental Mesonet, MRMS archive — radar-based rainfall estimates that fill in the latest day or two before gridMET is published.
  • NOAA GEFS — the ensemble-mean rain forecast for the week ahead.
  • NOAA National Water Prediction Service, SHIF1 — the official gauge page, and the National Water Model day-1 flows used in the track-record comparison.
  • National Weather Service, Melbourne and Osceola County Emergency Management — official flood warnings.
